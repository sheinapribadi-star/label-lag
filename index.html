A fraud model can look better than it is, because the truth is still in the mail. When a stolen card is used, it can take weeks for the cardholder to notice and file a dispute. Until that report arrives, the payment sits in your training data as a legitimate charge.

The idea comes from Scaling Radar: Fighting fraud with agent-assisted machine learning by Ryan Drapeau and Sai Samant (Stripe, 19 October 2026). In that post, a model trained on a much larger slice of the network looked better in early tests, then had to be rolled back once delayed fraud reports arrived.

Constructed sample, not Stripe data. Fraud is overrepresented so the lag is visible.

Fit now, with the labels you have

Train on every payment in a 10-week window, using only the reports that have arrived by training day. Recent weeks count more, so the model can chase new attacks. Those same weeks are where labels are least trustworthy.

Train only on mature weeks

Drop the most recent six weeks and train only on payments that are old enough for most disputes to have arrived. Labels are more trustworthy. The window is smaller, and a little stale.

A charge that looked safe

The Stripe post describes using model explanations to see which features pushed a score up or down on missed fraud. Below are the late-reported stolen-card charges from the evaluation week. Five of them were left through by the “fit now” rule. It had learned to give the hiding pattern, clean history elsewhere and a first charge here, less weight, because many recent charges with that pattern were still labeled legitimate when it trained.

How this sample was built

The evaluation week has 280 charges, of which 42 are fraud: 14 obvious card-testing cases that get reported quickly, and 28 stolen-card cases that usually take three to eight weeks to be reported. In live card payments, fraud is closer to 1 in 1,000. This sample is deliberately dense so a short demo can show the lag.

Both rules are logistic models scored at a frozen 2% false-positive operating point, chosen on the mature training weeks using only the reports that had arrived by training day. The metric I care about is the label-lag gap: apparent recall on day 0 minus recall once the labels have matured. A training rule holds up if that gap is small, and if mature recall stays high.

On this sample, the mature-weeks model caught every evaluation-week fraud case, and the “fit now” model missed five of the late-reported ones. It paid for that with more false alarms: 4 legitimate charges flagged, against 2 for “fit now,” out of 238. Both stay under the 2% target. A live system would not go 42 for 42. The sample is built so this failure can happen, so it shows the mechanism, not how often it happens in practice. The useful result is the gap, and the fact that the gap only appears after the disputes arrive.
